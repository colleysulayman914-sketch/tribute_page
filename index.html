A tribute

Alan Turing

Mathematician, codebreaker, and the person who gave computing its first clear idea of what a machine can decide.

23 June 1912 — 7 June 1954

Alan Turing at sixteen, photographed in 1928. Four years later he arrived at King’s College, Cambridge, already occupied with the question of what can be computed.

In 1936, at twenty-four, Turing described a machine that reads a tape, one symbol at a time, and follows a finite set of rules. That abstract device — the Turing machine — is still the measure of what an algorithm is. During the war he turned the same habits of mind on the German naval Enigma, and after it he asked whether a machine could think.

  1. 1912

    London

    Born Alan Mathison Turing in Maida Vale. His parents spent much of his childhood in India; he grew up in English schools, often alone with a problem.

  2. 1931

    Cambridge

    Elected a scholar of King’s College. He reads mathematics, rows, and begins the work that will become On Computable Numbers.

  3. 1936

    The machine

    Publishes the paper that defines computability. A procedure is computable if a Turing machine can carry it out. The halting problem shows that some questions have no general answer.

  4. 1939

    Bletchley Park

    Joins the Government Code and Cypher School. At Hut 8 he helps design the methods and machines — including the bombe — that read U-boat traffic. The work stays secret for decades.

  5. 1950

    Can machines think?

    In Computing Machinery and Intelligence he replaces the question with a test: if a conversation cannot tell person from program, treat the program as thinking. The Turing test is still argued over, and still used.

  6. 1952

    The prosecution

    Convicted of “gross indecency” for a relationship with another man. He chooses a course of synthetic oestrogen over prison. His security clearance, and much of his public work, ends.

  7. 1954

    Wilmslow

    Dies at forty-one. The inquest records suicide by cyanide. In 2009 the British government apologises. In 2013 he receives a royal pardon. In 2021 his portrait appears on the £50 note.

We can only see a short distance ahead, but we can see plenty there that needs to be done.

Alan Turing, 1950
